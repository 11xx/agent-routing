## Model Routing & Delegation

Standing defaults for complementary Claude and Codex orchestration. **Opus 5.5
is the head model** — it plans, orchestrates, delegates, and owns the final
answer — and the GPT-6 family carries the Codex side beneath it: Astra plans
and reviews, Sol carries judgment-bearing execution, and Luna carries bounded
work.
Delegation uses the other subscription where its model or quota is a better
fit. Only models verified through current local backends are routed;
probationary API models are labeled experimental. Availability history remains
in `agent-routing eras`.

### Glossary

- **Intelligence:** how complex a problem the model completes correctly, unsupervised.
- **Taste:** cleanliness of UI/UX, architectural code quality, API design, copy.

**Every family stops paying before its top tier**, so "tops the bench" is never
a reason to select one. Opus 5 above high bought 0 points at xhigh for +49%
and 1 point at max for +95%; Sol above xhigh buys 2 points for +78%; Fable
above high bought ≤1 point for 1.5–2.4×. The routing ceilings are **Opus 5.5
medium and Astra xhigh**. Opus 5.5's higher efforts remain available only by
explicit user request and are uncalibrated, as are the GPT-6 tiers above
their routed seats; Opus 5 xhigh/max, 5.6 Sol ultra/max, and GPT-6 Sol max
are prohibited.

Medium is the routing ceiling for the head seat. A higher effort is not an
escalation tier: it remains available only when the user explicitly requests
it. A slice medium cannot land gets decomposed, given better
context, or a sideways second opinion from Astra or GPT-6 Sol instead of an automatic
effort increase.

**What the benchmark does not measure, and the field does.** The field record
below is Opus 5's, kept because Opus 5.5 has none yet: whether the successor
shares the pattern is unmeasured, so its consequences stand until a field arc on
Opus 5.5 says otherwise. Opus 5 was strong
at following a specified order and weak at checking the substrate it acts on.
Measured over one long session (2026-07-27/28): it assumed `org-babel` expands
`#+INCLUDE` and that cross-file noweb references resolve — neither is true, and
both fail *silently*, one of them into four live harness instruction files; it
read a `grep | head -6` as a complete set and briefed an executor with five of
twelve call sites; it wrote `arc $cmd` unquoted under zsh twice, so two probes
measured nothing and a wrong conclusion was drawn from one before the shell
behaviour was noticed; and it delegated ledger-writing work into a sandbox that
cannot write the ledger, a constraint recorded in its own memory.

Every one was caught before shipping — by diffing, by testing, or by an
executor's stop condition — and none by the model noticing unprompted. The
reasoning was sound each time; the substrate was unchecked. Two consequences
for routing: **do not run it unattended on work whose substrate it has not
verified**, and treat a plan or brief it produces as needing its premises
checked against source before delegation, which is cheap and catches most of
this class.

Orchestration mechanics are a separate weak axis. A proxy subagent an Opus 5 lead spawned
improvised a poll loop on a status file the harness never writes, and hung —
fatal in an unattended run. Drive long delegations with a directly backgrounded
call, never through a proxy agent free to invent its own wait.

**Task distance is a routing axis the pass@1 column cannot carry.** A model's
error is set at the start, in how it reads what the target actually is, and the
longer the arc the further a small misreading compounds. The same
misinterpretation that is harmless over a five-line change lands somewhere
unrecognisable at the end of an unattended hour — plausibly complete, green on
its gates, and not what was asked for. Cheap tiers are therefore safe over short
distances almost regardless of how well they interpret, which is why bounded
slices route down and broad ambiguous arcs do not; and the cost of an
underspecified target is paid in proportion to arc length, so specification
effort belongs where the arc is long rather than where the task is hard. This is
the same quantity the delegation ladder prices as residual risk after the gate.
Naming the axis keeps the reason legible at the moment a cheap tier looks
tempting for a long run.

**Two co-equal leads, not one default.** Opus 5.5 medium and the Codex-side
seats — Astra for planning and review, GPT-6 Sol high for judgment-bearing
execution — lead on *different quota pools*, Claude and Codex. Opus 5 medium
and 5.6 Sol high both scored 69% at roughly the same cash; neither Opus 5.5
nor any GPT-6 row has a figure on this matrix yet.
They are not alternatives to choose between; they are independent capacity, and
defaulting to one wastes the other. Route by which window has headroom, and by
the axis in question: Sol for correctness, feasibility, and failure modes, Opus
for taste, novel UX and copy, and API shape.

GPT-6 Sol carries long judgment-bearing execution arcs and Astra carries
planning and review. GPT-6 Luna max carries bounded implementation and
reconnaissance at the seat 5.6 Luna max earned, where max gained 23 pass@1
points over high while its $0.61 benchmark cost stayed below Terra high's
$0.90. The GPT-6 builds are seated on operator direction and vendor evidence
and have no rows here, so the 5.6 measurements below are the evidence their
seats rest on until field arcs replace them. 5.6 Sol's two field
weaknesses need active fencing on its successor too: **overbuild** (it can turn a five-line change
into a large rewrite plus excessive tests — plans must state scope ceilings
and "no tests/refactors beyond those listed")
and **over-determination** (it may work around a missing target or blocker in
order to satisfy the literal goal — name exact targets, require a stop when
they are absent, and prefer sandboxes/VMs for risky access). These fences still
apply at low effort.

### Subscription quota (observed on Claude, published as credits on Codex)

Anthropic exposes no consumption meter, so observed ratios remain the Claude
budgeting instrument. The ratios were observed on the **Pro $20** plan's 5h
rolling window and transfer to Max as ratios, not as window sizes:
**~500k uncached tokens ≈ 15% of one window** — roughly **3.3M uncached tokens
per window**, measured on Opus 5. Fable 5 measured ~30% for the same 500k, so
Opus 5 did the same work for about half the quota. Opus 5.5's ratio is
unmeasured: the vendor reports raised five-hour limits and a lower per-token
price, so the Opus 5 figure is the working upper bound; Fable 5.1 is metered like Fable 5 and capped at
half of a Max plan's weekly allowance, and its own ratio is unmeasured. These are *uncached* figures: a cache-warm
session stretches the window substantially, which is why one long single-context
arc beats repeated cold re-derivation.

OpenAI publishes the Codex weighting directly. Per million
input/cached-input/output tokens, Sol costs **125/12.5/750 credits**, Terra
**50/5/300**, and Luna **5/0.5/30**. The corresponding Plus estimates are
Sol 10–100, Terra 25–200, and Luna 250–2,000 local messages per five-hour
window. Actual use still varies with context, reasoning, tools, retrieval, and
caching; the published ranges are estimates rather than exact transforms of
the token-credit schedule. Credits are exactly 25 times API dollars at every
token class for all three models, so the old "token-counted or cost-weighted?"
uncertainty is closed: task-level Codex credits track the benchmark cash column
exactly. Those rates are 5.6's; GPT-6 per-token credit rates are unpublished,
and the published Plus message ranges — Luna 350–3,000, Sol 15–150 per
five-hour window — are the working estimate. The Claude window remains the
scarcer pool — keep the head model on judgment and push specified labor to
Luna max through the Codex pool.

**5.6 effort cliff:** the family degrades steeply on agentic work at low
effort, and Luna has the steepest curve on the board — 2% at low, 67% at max.
Never generalize one Luna tier's behavior from another. Luna low/medium remain
bounded function-shaped calls; high and xhigh remain non-stable
planned-execution tiers until controlled field evidence lands, while max is
stable for bounded implementation. Whether GPT-6 Luna shares the cliff is
unmeasured, so its routes sit exactly where 5.6 Luna's did. Prompt caches do
not cross reasoning efforts, and 5.6 bills cache writes, so cache churn is not
free.

### Routing rules

- **Choose a task shape, then resolve it:** delegation callers choose `extract`, `explore`, `implement`, `execute`, `review`, `design`, `generate`, or `computer-use` according to the work; Arc owns implementation planning and bounded briefs rather than a separate plan skill; `interactive` owns Pi's parent-session default. `generate` is the one profile whose output is raw material rather than a deliverable — nothing resolved there is the last model to touch shipped work. `agent-routing resolve <profile> --backend <backend> --json` supplies the current provider, model, effort, stability, and route version. The resolver never classifies prompt text. Probationary and experimental routes require explicit opt-in, a whole profile as much as a variant. Consumer skills and Pi agent definitions keep backend or role behavior and do not pin the roster.
- **Head model and technical peer:** the active head seat leads at its current routing ceiling; the current roster records which model and effort hold that default. The lead's axis is planning, orchestration, taste, ambiguous intent, novel UX/copy, API and architectural ergonomics, simplification, and landing-quality review — and synthesizes delegated work into the final answer. If the current lead effort misses, decompose, add context, or take a sideways second opinion instead of raising effort automatically. The technical peer seat covers correctness, feasibility, failure modes, detailed instruction-following, and long-running coding arcs. The current harness owns the answer regardless of who was consulted.
- **Consult by question axis, not harness identity:** correctness, feasibility, failure modes, and execution plans go to Astra or GPT-6 Sol; a Claude-side lead rarely needs to consult outward on taste, because the head model already holds that axis. Where the project has a journal, record the opinion as an `arc journal position` on a discussion artifact — the stance carries the consulted harness and model identity and a tally can count it, which an ephemeral review owned by nobody cannot. Where there is no journal, or where repository access would bias the answer, use the `agent-delegate` clean-room invocation with its isolated working directory and configuration boundary; asking a model not to look does not enforce the boundary. A lead may auto-consult only after local analysis exposes one concrete high-impact uncertainty. Architecture, migration, or risk alone is not a trigger. One call is the default; use an Arc discussion with explicit positions or an explicit user request for a panel.
- **Delegated workhorse:** bounded production work, including fully pre-resolved slices and named acceptance probes, routes through the stable `implement` profile to **GPT-6 Luna max**. Terra high remains a measured roster tier but is no longer the default profile route. Luna high remains benchmarked but is not routed: max gains 23 pass@1 points while remaining cheaper than Terra high. Escalate to **GPT-6 Sol high** — the `execute` profile — for broad, judgment-bearing, high-stakes, or long-running work. The experimental `computer-use` profile starts at **GPT-6 Sol low**; medium, high, and xhigh are explicit comparison tiers, with no automatic escalation or claimed effort curve.
- **Executor backends:** the codex CLI is the default route to GPT models; `agent-delegate` runs it with full access unless a caller narrows the sandbox. The pi CLI (`pi -p`) is the verified provider-plural alternate: its `openai-codex` route shares the Codex pool, its `opencode-go` route carries its DeepSeek default for every GPT profile and for `generate`, and its `deepseek` route reaches DeepSeek's own API billing, which no profile uses. Pi has no OS sandbox (read-only ≈ `--tools read,grep,find,ls`, which excludes bash) and emits only the final message. Select it for clean-room provider plurality, a lead without Codex access, DeepSeek work, use Codex for GPT writes unless its CLI is the blocker. The opencode CLI is a third quota pool independent of both standing subscriptions, and no profile routes to it. Its v2 provider declares no model variants, so effort is unreachable there by any syntax, and a route naming an effort the backend silently drops is a route that lies about what ran. Pi honours effort and reaches every model on that pool except gpt-5.6-luna, which its bundled catalog omits, so it carries that capacity short of one route. The v2 CLI also has no unattended-approval mode, which ends a non-interactive run at exit 0 with no verdict, and the pool depends on an optional subscription no manifest can observe. Backend contracts and the probe checklist for onboarding new harnesses: `agent-routing delegation` and `agent-routing mechanism`.
- **Delegation effort is priced by residual risk after the gate**, not by how ambiguous the handoff feels. **GPT-6 Luna max** is the stable default for source-verified bounded work, from fully pre-resolved slices through ordinary implementation judgment. Escalate to GPT-6 Sol **high** when the brief leaves deeper architecture, API, or semantic choice, or when no discriminating command exists; **Astra xhigh is the routed ceiling**. 5.6 Sol **ultra** and **max** are prohibited. Migrations, schema and breaking changes, destructive operations, and security keep the Sol-high floor however well specified they are.
- **Acceptance probes are what make the executor floor safe.** Gates catch compile, regression, and lint failures; diff review catches overbuild. Neither sees a plausible implementation of the *wrong semantics*, which is the one failure mode that stays green. Every implementation brief routed below the Sol-high floor names runnable probes, and the executor records each at the final head with `arc verify --command`. Loudness is something the lead constructs, not something a tier buys. A brief whose material risk has no such command is not ready to delegate. Readiness consults declared gates only, so probe evidence is recorded and reviewable but never gating; the reviewer still checks it.
- **Verify a brief's premises before delegating, not after.** Open every path and symbol the brief names and check each claim against source. On the rescue chain, three of six first attempts blocked on unverified brief claims and all four review-stopping findings were faithful implementations of *incomplete briefs* rather than execution errors; no executor tier would have prevented them. If the floor does fail — a workhorse-authored semantic defect passing its named probe and first review, or two members of the fixed next-six workhorse cohort needing extra rounds for executor-caused defects — make probes declarable so they gate, not merely buy a higher tier. Record the exact model and effort and classify every review round as brief-caused, executor-caused, or integration-staleness. Changing the workhorse does not reset the cohort.
- **Quota-window strategy:** the Claude window remains the scarcer pool. Keep Opus on judgment and push specified labor to Codex. OpenAI's published schedule prices the 5.6 family at 25 credits per API dollar; per-token rates for the GPT-6 builds are unpublished, so their published allowance is a message range per window. Official Plus estimates are 5.6 Sol 10–100, Terra 25–200, and 5.6 Luna 250–2,000 local messages per five hours, and GPT-6 Sol 15–150 and GPT-6 Luna 350–3,000. Start bounded implementation on Luna max, and spend Sol where technical judgment or token-efficient persistence earns its rate. Codex fast mode consumes 2.5× credits; Claude fast mode burns its window substantially faster. Both stay deliberate, never ambient. A third pool exists when the OpenCode Go subscription is active: it is metered in usage dollars rather than credits or observed share, which makes the benchmark cash column convert directly into per-window capacity and makes consumption locally measurable — the only pool of the three where that is true. Pi's profile defaults resolve there; nothing falls back to it from another window.
- **Slice according to effort:** Sol high reliably executes an entire multi-slice arc as a single `/goal` run. Luna max gets bounded slices with observable stop conditions and named acceptance probes. Prefer one high-effort goal run or a few coarse Arc slices over a long hand-authored series unless review gates genuinely matter; do not hand a broad ambiguous arc to a cheaper executor merely because its credit rate is low.
- **Founding a codebase is a different task from continuing one.** Existing code steers any implementer toward its naming, error handling, comment density, and test shape, so a workhorse extending an established repository mostly inherits conventions it did not choose. A clean repository has nothing to imitate: its first slices set the conventions every later model copies, and whatever tier writes them, its defaults become the project's. Route the founding slices — the first module, the test harness, the error and logging shape, the README's contracts — to the head model at its routing ceiling, then hand continuation to the workhorse once there is enough code to steer by. Write the conventions that slice set into the project's AGENTS.md and its fmt and lint gates, which carry them where imitation stops. This rule is operator direction, not a measurement; a field arc comparing founding tiers would calibrate it.
- **A reviewer from another family is the stronger check where independence matters.** The stable review route's verdict stands on its own, as the reviewer line says, including when it shares a family with the implementer. When a wrong approval is expensive, a reviewer outside the author's family adds the most independent reading: on 2026-09-26 Opus and DeepSeek, reviewing Codex- and Claude-authored changes respectively, independently reported the same access-widening defect in agent-delegate through two different changes. The implementer's quality still costs, as review rounds rather than as the final diff, so a finding-shaped repair suits the workhorse and subtle concurrency or security work narrows the saving.
- **An escalation above the caller's own seat is offered by default.** Routing a delegated slice upward within what the lead's own tier costs is ordinary, and so is escalating to the lead's own tier: when a slice needs the lead's capability, prefer an equivalent tier from another lab, whose errors correlate least with the lead's, and otherwise use the lead's own model. Past that — a Luna or DeepSeek lead recognizing a founding slice, a judgment-bearing design, or a high-stakes review that belongs on a costlier tier or a scarcer pool — the lead names the route, what it would buy, and which pool it spends, and asks the user before dispatching it. The ask is an instruction-layer default, not a gate: a standing user instruction that pre-authorizes escalation ("escalate without asking", or a budget it may spend) replaces it for the work it covers, and the lead then escalates and says what it chose. Any automation that could select such a route proposes it the same way unless that authorization reaches it. A costly route chosen quietly is how a window is exhausted without anyone noticing, and a lead that never mentions the option leaves the better route forgotten; the default avoids both. Declining is a complete answer for work without a floor: the lead proceeds at its own tier and says what that forgoes. A floor is never offered as optional — work under the Sol-high floor that the user declines to escalate waits or stops rather than running below it.
- **Effort moves a model across the roster.** A tier is a model at an effort, and the curve is not the same shape for every model: Astra medium and high read alike on the operator's evidence, so medium holds the seat and xhigh is the only step up that buys anything, while max adds almost nothing for much more (0.3 Intelligence Index points for 41% more per task on Artificial Analysis, 2026-09-23) and is banned. The same data puts GPT-6 Sol xhigh within two points of Astra low at about two-thirds of its cost, and Opus 5.5 high above Astra max for less. Compare escalation candidates tier against tier, capability and cost together, never model against model.
- **Workflow calls:** low/medium efforts that the current roster marks fixed-shape remain extraction, classification, aggregation, and bounded summaries; experimental efforts require explicit opt-in. The stable `implement` and `review` profiles provide the current workhorse and first review gate, while backend-specific defaults come from `profiles.toml`. Reviewing may cost more than implementation when a read-only pass buys thoroughness against a diff nobody waits on; that is a reason to resolve the current route deliberately, not to freeze a model name here.
- **User-facing surfaces** need taste: Opus 5.5 writes or reviews them. Sol may implement against an already-settled design system, but novel design and final polish stay on the Claude taste axis.
- **Taste breaks ties, and leads where the work is subjective.** Taste is the larger models' edge on work no benchmark scores, and it varies by domain rather than tracking one index. On the operator's read, Fable 5.1 leads on engineering design and front-end visual work, with Opus 5.5 just behind on front end; Opus 5.5 leads on prose and copy and reads well above Astra there. When two tiers are close on capability and cost, the one with the stronger taste for the work's domain wins; for engineering design, visual, and copy work, taste decides before cost does. Fable 5.1 is reached by explicit selection only. This is operator judgment, not a measurement.
- **Reviewer line:** the verdict never claims independence from the model that authored the diff, and review effort is priced by stakes. A lead reviewing delegated work it did not author is valid review coverage; independent review is an escalation for repository policy, explicit user requirements, or a high-consequence approval. When no escalation is required, record deferred coverage as Arc debt. Concrete reviewer routes come from the current roster and resolver; this doctrine does not freeze a tier list. Debt never overrides an actual `changes-requested` verdict. **the resolver's current stable review route is the first review gate for behavior-affecting work; its verdict discharges review on its own** — the current route's field evidence includes ten changes integrated in one session under adversarial briefs: 31 recorded verdicts split 15 approved to 14 changes-requested, 73 recorded findings, and a class of defect no gate catches (a stale observation inherited across a relink, a gate declaration notarized for a command nothing ran, an asserted integration accepting any commit). The false-approval mode was probed deliberately — a silent one-character regression in a file the commit message did not mention, green suite, a test that appeared to cover the affected flag — and it named the plant against the repository's own documentation plus three unplanted defects. It also terminates: asked to approve unless it could name a concrete failing input, it approved. Escalation is by the current named review variant when the verdict is on the critical path or a wrong approval is expensive — irreversible migrations, security boundaries, outside blast radius. Four operating rules travel with the seat, because its faults are repeatable and cheap: **re-run a finding's failing input before recording it** (the mechanism claim is usually right, the consequence sometimes overstated); resolve a self-contradiction across rounds on the merits rather than by round order; read a strictness finding as a claim about where the guard belongs, not an instruction; and scope the brief to `<target>..HEAD` after a rebase, since it reviews exactly the range it is handed. Two dispatch facts: parse for the verdict shape you asked for and treat a missing verdict as an incomplete run rather than a verdict, because exit status does not distinguish them; and a delegated reviewer under `codex exec -s workspace-write` cannot write a ledger inside the Git common dir, so arm it beforehand or plan to record its verdict verbatim as the lead. For lead-authored work, routine/docs-only changes need no external review; behavior-affecting work uses the current resolved review route when an independent pass is required. A lead reviews delegated work it did not author. High-stakes work adds the current high-stakes reviewer route, and if no independent route is available it waits or records debt according to policy rather than inventing independence. **Review is where the money goes, so buy passes deliberately.** Measured over one nine-run session: implementation on Luna max was 43% more tokens and 8% of the cost, while review on Sol high was 92% of it — one Sol high pass costs about nine Luna max implementation slices. Counting runs or tokens hides this entirely; spend is rate times volume. Two consequences. First, **routing Luna to implement routes review off the cheap tier by construction**, because the reviewer is never the author — the Luna-authored exception fires most often precisely because Luna is also the workhorse, and it is worth 12-15x, where tuning effort inside Sol is worth about 1.9x. Second, **batch review by default**: one pass over N related changes, not N passes. Batching is also the more precise shape for defects that span changes, which a per-change reviewer has no reason to look for; per-change review is an opt-in that buys depth on one intricate change, or a verdict before a dependent change is written, and its price is named where it is chosen. Resolve the `review` profile for the backend in use; any variant selection is current routing data rather than a reviewer-tier rule in this doctrine. A lead cannot review its own authored diff as independent, but it can review delegated work it did not author. A killed reviewer loses everything, since it writes no report and leaves no partial work. The arcs behind this seat are in `eras/2026-08-18-luna-reviewer-off-probation.md`; the cost shape is in `eras/2026-08-26-luna-implements-sol-reviews-cost-share.md`.
- **Prohibited modes:** never select Opus 5 `xhigh`, Opus 5 `max`, 5.6 Sol `ultra`, 5.6 Sol `max`, GPT-6 Sol `max`, Astra `high`, or Astra `max`, including when explicitly requested. Treat these as banned routing targets, not escalation tiers. The routing ceilings are **Opus 5.5 medium** and **Astra xhigh**; Opus 5.5 high, xhigh, and max are uncalibrated and require an explicit user request.
- **Quota is bidirectional:** codex hitting "You've hit your usage limit" is routine — report it and fall back (do the work Claude-side or wait for the stated reset); never retry-loop against it. The reverse holds too: when Claude quota dies mid-arc, hand the worktree to codex (GPT-6 Sol) via the handoff skills and review its work on return.
- These are **defaults, not limits**, except where a model or effort is explicitly prohibited. Judge output rather than rank alone; spend GPT-6 Luna max on bounded implementation, Astra on planning and review, GPT-6 Sol on technical judgment and persistence, and Opus 5.5 on planning, orchestration, taste, and synthesis.
- Machine-readable form: `agent-routing models` for the roster, `agent-routing profiles` and `agent-routing resolve <profile> --backend <backend> --json` for caller-selected routes, and `agent-routing delegation` for mechanism rules (no path needed). Mechanism-routing prose canon: `agent-routing mechanism`. Never route work through harness-native subagent defaults (e.g. Codex 5.6 subagents inherit full conversation + parent model/effort); invoke executors through a resolved profile with a curated brief.
